# Why Coding Matters to Artists and Designers

## Introduction

Multimedia entertainment lies at the intersection of creative practice, digital technology, and audience experience. As postgraduate students in innovative multimedia entertainment, we are expected not only to imagine compelling ideas but also to understand how they can be realised. Among the technical skills available to creative practitioners, coding can initially seem distant from artistic practice. However, it is increasingly important for artists and designers working with interactive media. Coding is not simply a production skill; it can provide creative agency, enable new forms of expression, support interdisciplinary collaboration, and develop critical literacy. Drawing on my observations of interactive theme-park attractions and the structure of game-development teams, this essay argues that a working knowledge of code helps artists and designers become more independent, informed, and thoughtful creators.

## Coding as Creative Agency

Digital tools evolve rapidly. New game engines, creative software packages, and interactive devices appear every year, often making it tempting for artists and designers to treat technology as a black box. We select presets, adjust sliders, and work within systems designed by others. While these tools are useful, relying entirely on them can limit creative choices to what their developers have anticipated.

Coding changes this relationship. When an artist can write a script, develop a shader, or design simple interaction logic, the computer becomes a more flexible creative material rather than a fixed instrument. The practitioner is no longer only a user of software but also someone capable of adapting or extending it. Reas and Fry (2014) argue that programming can give visual artists new ways to construct images, processes, and systems. In this sense, coding offers creative agency: it gives makers greater control over both the process and the outcome of their work.

This does not mean that every artist needs to become a professional software engineer. Instead, even a basic understanding of coding can help creative practitioners recognise the possibilities and limitations of digital tools, allowing them to make more intentional decisions.

## Coding as a New Expressive Medium

Coding is not only a more efficient method for producing existing artistic forms. It also enables forms of expression that depend on rules, behaviour, change, and participation. Generative art, procedural animation, responsive visual systems, and interactive installations all rely on code to create experiences that can evolve in real time. As Shiffman (2021) demonstrates through computational simulations and creative coding examples, code can be used to model movement, randomness, natural systems, and interactions that are difficult to achieve through static design methods alone.

My own experience of visiting theme-park interactive attractions made this especially clear. Some of the most memorable moments involved environments that reacted to visitors: projections responded to body movement, sounds changed as groups entered a space, or a scene developed differently depending on audience choices. What made these experiences compelling was not simply their visual quality, but the sense that the environment was responding to the audience. Static graphics software can create the visual assets for such an attraction, but code is needed to produce the responsive behaviour that makes the experience interactive.

For artists and designers, therefore, coding can become an expressive medium in its own right. It makes it possible to create systems rather than only images, and experiences rather than only objects. In multimedia entertainment, where novelty and audience engagement are highly valued, this ability can help practitioners develop ideas beyond the limits of off-the-shelf tools.

## Coding as a Bridge for Collaboration

A practical reason for artists and designers to learn coding is that it can improve collaboration in interdisciplinary teams. This is particularly visible in the game-development industry, where Technical Artists often work between art and engineering teams. Their role involves translating artistic goals into technically workable solutions while communicating technical constraints back to artists and designers.

The existence of this role suggests that connecting creative and technical concerns is an important need within game-development teams. However, Technical Artists should not be seen as a reason for other creatives to avoid coding completely. A Technical Artist can help solve implementation and communication problems, but designers still need enough technical understanding to make informed decisions during the earliest stages of a project.

For example, a designer without any knowledge of technical feasibility may propose an interaction that requires more time, equipment, or processing power than the project can support. If this limitation is only identified after a concept has been presented to clients or included in a production schedule, changing it may be difficult and costly. By contrast, a designer with basic coding knowledge is more likely to consider technical constraints while developing an idea. Their conversation with engineers can move from “Can this be made?” to “What is the most effective way to make this?” This shared understanding can reduce misunderstanding, save time, and improve the final outcome.

The same principle applies beyond games. Mobile applications, web experiences, immersive exhibitions, and augmented-reality installations all depend on software. Although creative practitioners do not need to implement every technical feature themselves, understanding the logic behind digital systems helps them communicate more effectively with the people who do.

## Coding as Critical Literacy

Coding also matters because it encourages critical awareness of the technologies that shape contemporary media. Digital tools are not neutral: their interfaces, algorithms, and defaults reflect particular assumptions about what is efficient, valuable, or possible. Artists and designers who understand some of the underlying logic of these systems are better able to question how they operate and whom they serve.

Andersen and Goriunova (2017) emphasise that software is connected to culture, pleasure, and power rather than being merely a neutral technical tool. Learning code can therefore help creative practitioners move beyond using technology uncritically. It allows them to ask why a system behaves in a particular way, what kinds of choices it encourages, and what experiences or users it may exclude. This kind of critical literacy is particularly important in an era when algorithms and artificial intelligence increasingly influence creative production and audience experience.

## Conclusion

Coding matters to artists and designers because it expands what they can imagine, make, and question. It provides creative agency by helping practitioners move beyond the limitations of ready-made tools. It enables interactive, generative, and responsive forms of expression that static methods alone cannot produce. It also supports stronger collaboration by allowing designers to make more informed decisions and communicate more effectively with technical teams. Finally, coding develops critical literacy by making the hidden systems behind digital media more visible.

For creative practitioners, learning code is not about replacing artistic intuition with technical thinking. It is about combining the two. When artists and designers can understand both creative vision and the systems that bring it to life, they are better equipped to create multimedia experiences that are technically feasible, meaningful, and engaging for audiences.
