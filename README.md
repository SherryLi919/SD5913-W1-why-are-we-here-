# Why Coding Matters to Artists and Designers

## Introduction

Multimedia entertainment sits at the intersection of creative art, digital technology, and audience experience. As postgraduate students in innovative multimedia entertainment, we are trained to be both imaginative creators and competent makers. Among all the technical skills we are expected to learn, coding is often the one that artists and designers feel most distant from — and yet it may be the most transformative. This essay reflects on why coding matters to creative practitioners, arguing that it is not merely a production skill but a source of creative agency, a new expressive medium, a bridge for interdisciplinary collaboration, and a foundation for critical literacy. The discussion draws on personal observation of theme-park interactive attractions and on the structure of the game development industry.

## Coding as Creative Agency

Technology evolves extremely fast. New tools, game engines, and interactive devices appear every year. It is tempting for artists and designers to treat software as a black box: we press buttons, drag sliders, and rely on preset templates to produce visuals. Without coding knowledge, we are limited to what someone else has decided a tool should do. Coding changes this relationship fundamentally. It turns creative practitioners from passive consumers of software into active builders of their own creative systems. When an artist can write a script, a shader, or an interaction logic, the computer stops being a fixed instrument and becomes a malleable material — like clay or paint, but with infinite possibilities. This shift from user to maker is what gives coding its deepest value: it returns agency to the creator.

## Coding as a New Expressive Medium

Coding is not only a faster way to produce existing forms; it is a medium that enables forms that would otherwise be impossible. Generative art, procedural animation, real-time interactive installations, and responsive visual systems all depend on code. A designer who can programme does not have to wait for a software update to realise an idea; they can prototype it themselves. In an industry where novelty and originality are highly valued, the ability to create something that no off-the-shelf tool can produce is a genuine competitive advantage. From my own experience visiting theme-park interactive attractions, I observed that the most memorable moments — the ones visitors talk about afterwards — are almost always interactive: a projection that responds to movement, a scene that changes based on a collective decision, an environment that adapts to the crowd. None of these experiences can be designed in a static graphics editor; they are born in code.

## Coding as a Collaboration Bridge

Perhaps the most practical reason for artists and designers to learn coding is that it reduces the cost of collaboration in interdisciplinary teams. This is vividly illustrated by the game development industry, where a specialised role exists precisely to bridge the gap between art and engineering: the Technical Artist, or TA. The TA's job is to translate artistic intentions into technical solutions and to communicate technical constraints back to the art team. The very existence of this role is evidence that the communication gap between artists and programmers is real, persistent, and expensive enough to warrant a full-time intermediary.

One might object that the TA role proves the opposite — that the industry solution is to hire a specialist rather than to expect artists to learn code. This objection misses an important distinction. A TA can solve translation problems, but cannot solve judgment problems. A designer who has no understanding of code may, during the concept phase, propose an interaction that is technically prohibitively expensive or even impossible to implement. By the time the TA or engineering team feeds this back, the design may already be locked in presentations, milestones, or client expectations, making revision costly and politically difficult. A designer who understands code, by contrast, can internalise technical feasibility from the earliest stages of ideation. Their collaboration with engineers and TAs shifts from "can you make this work?" to "how should we make this work best?" — a qualitative difference that saves time, reduces friction, and produces better outcomes.

This principle extends beyond games. Any digital, interactive product — whether a mobile application, a web experience, an immersive exhibition, or an AR installation — requires code to function. The more a designer understands about how that code works, the less likely their design is to die in the handover to engineering.

## Coding as Critical Literacy

Finally, coding matters because it cultivates critical awareness. Digital media are not neutral tools; they encode assumptions, biases, and logics in their algorithms. An artist or designer who cannot read code is forced to accept these hidden logics at face value. Learning code demystifies the systems we work with, allowing us to question why a tool behaves the way it does, what it prioritises, and what it excludes. This critical stance is essential for creators who want their work to be thoughtful rather than merely decorative.

## Conclusion

In summary, coding matters to artists and designers for four interconnected reasons. It grants creative agency by freeing practitioners from the constraints of ready-made tools. It opens up an entirely new expressive medium that makes interactive and generative work possible. It reduces collaboration costs by enabling designers to communicate with engineers on shared ground and to make informed feasibility judgments early — a need made visible by the existence of the Technical Artist role in game development. And it fosters critical literacy by demystifying the algorithmic systems that underpin digital media. We learn technical methods, but more importantly, we develop the judgement to understand both what modern technology can do and what it should do. By combining creative vision with the ability to build, we can produce multimedia work that is not only technically polished, but also meaningful for audiences.
